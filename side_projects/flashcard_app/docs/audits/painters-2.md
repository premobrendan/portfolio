# Painters audit — cards 29–56

Scope: array indices 28–55 (Van Gogh through Mary Cassatt), 28 cards. Every bio paragraph, all 28 × 15 = 420 table rows, and all 28 `primary` fields were checked. Image URLs were not checked.

## Factual errors

- **Salvador Dalí** — "Expelled from the Surrealist group in 1934 over his politics and self-promotion" → He was **not** expelled in 1934. He was put on "trial" by the group late in 1934 and *narrowly avoided* expulsion; Breton announced his actual expulsion in the May 1939 issue of *Minotaure*, after Dalí praised Franco, Catholicism and the Falange. Suggested fix: "Tried by the Surrealist group in 1934 and expelled in 1939 over his politics and self-promotion". (Verified: Wikipedia *Salvador Dalí*, "Later in 1934, Dalí was subjected to a 'trial', in which he narrowly avoided being expelled" and "In the May issue of the Surrealist magazine Minotaure, André Breton announced Dalí's expulsion".)

- **Mark Rothko** — "before arriving in 1949 at the multiform paintings for which he is known: two or three softly bounded rectangles of luminous colour floating on a stained ground" → Terminology is inverted. The "multiforms" are the **transitional 1946–48** canvases of loose, large colour regions; they *developed into* the signature two-or-three-rectangle format, which he arrived at by 1949 and which is called his classic or sectional style. Calling the mature works "the multiform paintings" is wrong, and the works of 1949 are precisely *not* multiforms. Suggested fix: "…moved through figuration, Surrealist myth-making and the transitional 'multiforms' before arriving by 1949 at the paintings for which he is known". (Verified: Wikipedia *Mark Rothko*, "In 1946, Rothko created what art critics have since termed his transitional 'multiform' paintings" and "The 'multiforms' brought Rothko to a realization of his signature style".)

- **Diego Rivera** — Table row "| Man at the Crossroads | 1934 |" → **1933**. The fresco was painted in 1933 and ordered plastered over in May 1933; it was peeled off in February 1934. The 1934 date belongs to the Mexico City repainting, which carries a different title, *Man, Controller of the Universe*. (Verified: Wikipedia *Man at the Crossroads* — "Man at the Crossroads (1933) was a fresco… In May 1933, Rockefeller ordered [it] to be plastered over… The fresco was peeled off in 1934".) Note the bio's "notorious in 1934 when his Rockefeller Center mural was destroyed" is fine as written — only the table year is wrong.

- **Georgia O'Keeffe** — "Active from 1915, when her abstract charcoals were shown by the photographer Alfred Stieglitz" → The charcoals were **made** in late 1915; Stieglitz **showed** ten of them at 291 in **April 1916**. The card fuses two different years into one. Suggested fix: "Active from 1915, when she made the abstract charcoals that Alfred Stieglitz — whom she later married — exhibited at his 291 gallery the following year". (Verified: Wikipedia *Georgia O'Keeffe*, "the charcoal drawings she produced in 1915… Alfred Stieglitz… held an exhibit of her works in 1916" and "In April that year, Stieglitz exhibited ten of her drawings at 291".)

- **Jacob Lawrence** — Table rows "| The Life of Harriet Tubman | 1938–1939 |" and "| The Life of Frederick Douglass | 1939–1940 |" → The two are **swapped**: the Frederick Douglass series is 1938–39 (32 panels) and the Harriet Tubman series is 1939–40 (31 panels). (Verified: Wikipedia *Frederick Douglass*, "In 1938–39, African-American artist Jacob Lawrence created The Frederick Douglass series". Caution for whoever applies this: Wikipedia's own *Jacob Lawrence* article states the reverse — "the lives of Harriet Tubman (1938–39) and Frederick Douglass (1939–40)" — so the card is faithfully reproducing an error in one of its cited sources. The Hampton University Museum, which holds both series, and Wheat's catalogue *The Frederick Douglass and Harriet Tubman Series of 1938–40* give Douglass first.)

- **Egon Schiele** — "His unflinching nudes and gaunt self-portraits made him a founder of Expressionism" → Overstated to the point of being wrong. Expressionism was underway by 1905 (Die Brücke), when Schiele was fifteen; he is an **early exponent** of it, and a leading figure of *Austrian* Expressionism, not a founder. Suggested fix: "…made him one of the movement's most uncompromising figures" or "…an early exponent of Expressionism". (Verified: Wikipedia *Egon Schiele*, "mark the artist as an early exponent of Expressionism".)

- **Joan Miró** — "his playful abstraction strongly influenced American painters such as Gorky, Pollock and Calder" → **Alexander Calder was a sculptor**, not a painter, and is the odd man out in a list of painters. Suggested fix: drop Calder, or recast as "American artists such as Gorky, Pollock and Calder". (No lookup needed; Calder is universally classed as a sculptor — mobiles, stabiles.)

- **Jackson Pollock** — "The resulting all-over webs made him the first American painter of international stature" → False as stated. Whistler, Sargent and Cassatt all had international standing decades earlier, and Cassatt appears in this very pack as an American who exhibited with the Paris Impressionists in 1879. The defensible claim is about the movement, not the man. Suggested fix: "made him the first American painter to be treated in Europe as the leader of a major modern movement", or "made Abstract Expressionism the first American movement to win international influence".

- **Zhang Daqian** — Table row "| Ai Hen Hu (Aafchen See) | 1968 |" → "Aafchen See" is not a word in any language; it is a corrupted rendering of **Achensee**, the Austrian lake the picture depicts (愛痕湖, "Lake of Love's Traces"). Suggested fix: "Ai Hen Hu (Achensee)". (Verified: the same typo appears verbatim in Wikipedia's *Chang Dai-chien* works list — `1968 "Aafchen See" (《愛痕湖》)` — so this is inherited from the source, but it should not be reproduced.)

## Questionable / needs a second look

- **Gustav Klimt** — "active from the 1880s until his death in the influenza pandemic" — Klimt died 6 February 1918, of pneumonia after a stroke on 11 January. The 1918 pandemic's first recognised European wave is dated to March 1918, so a February death is at best contested as a pandemic death. Wikipedia says only "having suffered from a stroke and pneumonia". Safer: "until his death from a stroke and pneumonia in 1918".

- **Gustav Klimt** — "His mature golden phase… combined flat ornamental gold leaf with naturalistic faces and openly erotic allegory, provoking scandal over his University of Vienna ceiling paintings" — the causal chain is misleading. The University paintings (*Philosophy*, *Medicine*, *Jurisprudence*, 1900–07) are not gold-leaf works, and the scandal preceded/overlapped rather than resulted from the golden phase. The two claims should be separate sentences.

- **Henri Matisse** — "he invented the cut-out gouaches découpées" — two problems. (a) "cut-out gouaches découpées" is a tautology, since *gouaches découpées* means cut-out gouaches. (b) "invented" is too strong: his first recorded use of cut paper was 1919 for *Le chant du rossignol*, with another group in 1937–38; what happened after the 1941 surgery was that he developed it into an independent medium. Suggested fix: "he made the cut-out (*gouaches découpées*) his principal medium". (Verified: Wikipedia *Henri Matisse*, "his first recorded use of the technique was in 1919… it was only after his operation that… Matisse began to develop the cut-out technique as its own form".)

- **Henri Matisse** — "turning to art at twenty while recovering from appendicitis" — he began painting in 1889, when he was 19 (born 31 December 1869). "at twenty" is the common shorthand and is defensible, but "at nineteen" or "at twenty, in 1889/90" is more accurate.

- **Andy Warhol** — "born in Pittsburgh to Slovak immigrant parents" — his parents were **Rusyn (Lemko)**, from Miková in what is now northeastern Slovakia but was then Austria-Hungary. Wikipedia: "working-class Rusyn emigrants from Mikó, Austria-Hungary (now Miková in northeast Slovakia)". Suggested fix: "to Rusyn immigrant parents from what is now Slovakia".

- **Andy Warhol** — "he turned in 1962 to paintings of soup cans, dollar bills and movie stars" — he turned to Pop painting in **1961** (comic-strip and advertisement paintings, five of which were shown in the Bonwit Teller window in April 1961); 1962 is when the soup cans debuted. The named works are all 1962, so it half-works, but "turned in 1961 to fine art, and in 1962 to paintings of soup cans…" is cleaner.

- **Mary Cassatt** — "she was invited by Degas to exhibit with the Impressionists in 1879" — Degas issued the invitation in **1877**; she first showed with the group in 1879. As written, the date attaches to the invitation. Suggested fix: "invited by Degas in 1877, she first exhibited with the Impressionists in 1879".

- **Edvard Munch** — "a breakdown in 1908 returned him to Norway" — the breakdown was autumn 1908 but he spent eight months in a Copenhagen clinic and returned to Norway in **1909**. Compressed to the point of being slightly wrong.

- **Zhang Daqian** — "He left China in 1949 for Argentina, Brazil, California and finally Taipei" — he left in 1949 but did not reach Argentina until **1952** (Brazil 1954, California 1967, Taipei 1978). "left China in 1949, settling in turn in Argentina, Brazil, California and finally Taipei" avoids implying he went straight there.

- **Jacob Lawrence** — Table row "| The Life of John Brown | 1941 |" — Wikipedia's *Jacob Lawrence* article gives the 22-panel John Brown series as **1941–42**, while the Detroit Institute of Arts (which owns it) gives 1941. Worth picking one and citing it, since the neighbouring series in the same table all carry ranges.

- **Mark Rothko** — Table row "| Untitled (Black on Grey) | 1970 |" — the Guggenheim, which owns the best-known canvas of this group, dates it **1969–70**, and spells it "Gray". Minor, but this is exactly the series-spanning-years case the table should be careful about.

- **Piet Mondrian** — `primary` is "Broadway Boogie Woogie, Victory Boogie Woogie, The Gray Tree". *Victory Boogie Woogie* was unfinished at his death and is not among his best-known works to a general audience. *Composition with Red, Blue and Yellow* — his single most reproduced image, and already row 1 of the table — belongs in `primary` instead.

- **Jackson Pollock** — `primary` is "Blue Poles, Full Fathom Five, Guardians of the Secret". *Guardians of the Secret* (1943) is pre-drip and *Full Fathom Five* (1947) transitional; neither is a well-known Pollock outside specialist contexts. *Autumn Rhythm*, *No. 5, 1948* or *Lavender Mist* — all already in the table — would be truer to "well-known pieces".

- **Mark Rothko** — `primary` is "Seagram murals, Rothko Chapel, Black on Maroon". This is internally redundant: *Black on Maroon* **is** one of the Seagram murals (Tate). And the Rothko Chapel is a building/installation, not a work in the same sense. Two of the three slots effectively name one commission. Suggest replacing one with *Orange, Red, Yellow* or *No. 61 (Rust and Blue)*.

- **Jean-Michel Basquiat** — the table lists "| Untitled (Skull) | 1981 |" and "| Untitled | 1982 |" as separate rows. The 1982 "Untitled" is also a skull painting (the $110.5m Yusaku Maezawa canvas); two adjacent rows both effectively titled "Untitled" is unusable as a study prompt. Disambiguate the second, e.g. "Untitled (1982, Maezawa collection)".

- **Zhang Daqian** — several table titles are generic English renderings that cannot be pinned to a specific work ("Splashed-Colour Landscape", "Secluded Valley", "Swiss Peaks", "Lotus and Mandarin Ducks"). They match Wikipedia's works list exactly, so they are not invented, but they are not verifiable as distinct paintings either. Flagging as a body, not individually.

- **Whole set (28 cards)** — the column header is "Painting" but a substantial number of rows are not paintings: Klimt's *Stoclet Frieze* (mosaic); Toulouse-Lautrec's *Moulin Rouge: La Goulue*, *Divan Japonais*, *Jane Avril*, *Ambassadeurs: Aristide Bruant*, *May Milton* (lithograph posters); Matisse's *Chapelle du Rosaire de Vence* (a building) and *The Snail*/*Icarus*/*Blue Nudes* (cut-outs); Chagall's *Ceiling of the Palais Garnier* and *America Windows* (ceiling, stained glass); Dalí's *Lobster Telephone* (sculpture); Miró's *Woman and Bird* (sculpture) and *The Wall of the Moon and The Wall of the Sun* (ceramic murals); Rothko's *Rothko Chapel* (installation); Warhol's *Brillo Box* (sculpture); Lawrence's series (multi-panel sets). Not errors of attribution, but "Work" would be a more honest header.

## Prose issues

- **Henri de Toulouse-Lautrec** — "Working from acid colour, whiplash contour and blunt caricature drawn from Japanese prints and Degas, he raised the colour lithograph poster to fine art before dying at thirty-six of alcoholism and syphilis." — does not parse on first read. "Working from" governs three nouns, then "drawn from Japanese prints and Degas" attaches ambiguously to all or one of them, and the sentence then swerves into his death. Split it in two.

- **Diego Rivera** — "Married to Frida Kahlo, an active communist, he became notorious in 1934 when his Rockefeller Center mural was destroyed over a portrait of Lenin." — the appositive "an active communist" sits immediately after Kahlo and reads as describing her, when it is meant to describe Rivera. Move it: "A committed communist married to Frida Kahlo, he became notorious…".

- **Edward Hopper** — "His silent, unpeopled or barely peopled interiors made him the great poet of American loneliness and a lasting influence on film and photography." — three faults. "unpeopled or barely peopled" is clumsy and hedges itself away to nothing; it contradicts the preceding sentence's diners and theatres and his best-known works (*Nighthawks*, *Automat*, *Chop Suey* all have figures); and "the great poet of American loneliness" is the exact gushing register the brief asks to flag. Try "His stilled interiors and half-empty rooms…".

- **Salvador Dalí** — "He also worked in film, jewellery, sculpture and shameless showmanship." — the zeugma is a joke, and it lands the card in a register no other card in the set uses. "He also worked in film, jewellery and sculpture, and was a tireless self-publicist" keeps the point without the wink.

- **Georges Seurat** — "born in Paris and academically trained, who died of illness at only thirty-one" — "died of illness" says nothing (the cause is genuinely uncertain, variously given as diphtheria, meningitis or pneumonia), and "at only thirty-one" editorialises. "who died at thirty-one, of an illness never firmly identified" or simply "who died at thirty-one".

- **Paul Gauguin** — "though his colonial conduct is now heavily criticised" — euphemistic to the point of evasion. The criticism concerns his sexual relationships with Tahitian girls as young as thirteen, not "conduct" in general. Either say what it was or drop the clause; as written it gestures at a controversy without naming it.

- **Vincent van Gogh** — "he became the archetype of the tormented modern artist" — cliché, and it is the card's closing note, which gives it more weight than it earns.

- **Pablo Picasso** — "is the twentieth century's best-known political painting" — an unhedged superlative. "among the best-known political paintings of the twentieth century" costs nothing.

- **Mary Cassatt** — "Her Japanese-inspired colour prints of the 1890s are among the finest of the era" — evaluative filler; "of the era" is doing no work. Also "the only American formally to join the group" has the adverb wedged awkwardly before the infinitive; "the only American who formally joined the group" reads better.

- **Zhang Daqian** — "one of the most prodigious artists of the twentieth century" — hollow: prodigious in what respect? The source phrase is "one of the best-known and most prodigious *Chinese* artists"; dropping "Chinese" makes an already vague claim vaguer. Also "celebrated as a master copyist and forger of the old masters" — "celebrated as a… forger" is an odd pairing; "notorious as" or "renowned for his copies and forgeries" is what is meant.

- **Jean-Michel Basquiat** — "as half of the graffiti tag SAMO" — one is half of a *duo*, not half of a *tag*. "as half of the duo behind the SAMO tag".

- **Egon Schiele** — "taken up as a teenager by his mentor Gustav Klimt" — circular: Klimt became his mentor *by* taking him up, so the sentence presupposes its own outcome. "taken up as a teenager by Gustav Klimt, who became his mentor".

- **Andy Warhol** — "He blurred fine art, advertising and celebrity culture, and survived an assassination attempt in 1968." — a claim about his artistic significance and a biographical event yoked with a bare "and". They belong in separate sentences.

- **Marc Chagall** — "floating lovers, fiddlers, cows and shtetl rooftops through dreamlike, saturated colour" — "floating X through colour" is a strained transitive; "setting… adrift in" or "floating… in" would work.

- **Wifredo Lam** — "The Jungle, painted in 1943, is his defining statement" — "painted in 1943" duplicates the table row directly beneath it. No other card restates a date this way.

- **Stock phrase across the set** — "Active from…" opens or occupies a clause in 17 of these 28 bios (Van Gogh, Gauguin, Seurat, Klimt, Munch, Matisse, Picasso, Mondrian, Chagall, Klee, Dalí, Miró, Kahlo, O'Keeffe, Hopper, Rothko, Schiele, Cassatt). "Active from about X until his death" alone recurs in Van Gogh, Klimt and Kahlo. If the template is deliberate it is at least worth varying the wording; if not, several cards would read better with the active period folded into the following clause. Kandinsky is the one card in this half that carries **no** active-period statement at all, which is an inconsistency in the other direction.

- **Spelling** — the bios are consistently British (colour, civilisation, criticised, recognised, jewellery); no US/UK mixing there. The table titles are inconsistent, but that is because they follow the owning institutions: Seurat's "Entrance to the Harbor" and Mondrian's four "Gray" titles (US) sit beside O'Keeffe's "Grey Line" and Rothko's "Black on Grey". Zhang Daqian's "Splashed-Colour Landscape" is the one case where an Anglicised spelling has been imposed on a source title that reads "Splashed-color". Low priority; using the owning museum's title spelling throughout is the rule to state.
