# Painters audit — cards 1–28

Pack `untitled-pack-nzijzn` ("Painters"), array indices 0–27, Leonardo da Vinci through Paul Cézanne.
Baseline: the pack is in good shape. All 28 sets of birth/death years, nationalities, movements and
active periods check out, and the overwhelming majority of the 420 table rows carry correct
attributions and defensible dates. The findings below are the exceptions.

## Factual errors

- **Leonardo da Vinci** — table row `| The Baptism of Christ | c. 1475 |` listed flat among Leonardo's
  paintings. The picture is Andrea del Verrocchio's (Uffizi); Leonardo was the apprentice who painted
  the left-hand angel and much of the background. → Drop it, or relabel "The Baptism of Christ
  (Verrocchio, with Leonardo)". (Wikipedia, *The Baptism of Christ (Verrocchio and Leonardo)*:
  "finished around 1475 in the studio of ... Verrocchio and generally ascribed to him and his pupil
  Leonardo".)

- **Hieronymus Bosch** — table row `| Christ Carrying the Cross | c. 1510 |`. The Ghent painting is no
  longer given to Bosch: Wikipedia's infobox artist field reads "a follower of [[Hieronymus Bosch]]",
  short description "Painting by a follower of Hieronymus Bosch", dated c. 1510–1535 — i.e. possibly
  two decades after Bosch died in 1516. → Remove, or mark "follower of Bosch". Sits badly beside the
  card's own careful line that "Only about twenty-five surviving works are accepted as his."

- **Pieter Bruegel the Elder** — table row `| Landscape with the Fall of Icarus | c. 1560 |`. Since the
  1996 technical examination the Brussels attribution is "regarded as very doubtful"; the panel is
  "now usually seen as a good early copy by an unknown artist of Bruegel's lost original" (Wikipedia,
  *Landscape with the Fall of Icarus*). → Mark "after Bruegel" or drop.

- **Caspar David Friedrich** — table row `| Two Men Contemplating the Moon | 1825–1830 |`. That range
  is the Metropolitan Museum's later replica. The canonical picture, Dresden Galerie Neue Meister, is
  1819–20 (Wikipedia infobox year = 1819–1820). → 1819–1820.

- **Rembrandt** — "he produced roughly three hundred paintings, three hundred etchings and a celebrated
  sequence of **some eighty self-portraits** spanning forty years". Read in that list the eighty scans
  as paintings, and that is the discredited older count inflated by studio copies. Wikipedia:
  "At one time, approximately 90 paintings were counted as Rembrandt self-portraits but it is now known
  that he had his students copy his own self-portraits ... Modern scholarship has reduced the autograph
  count to over forty paintings, as well as a few drawings and thirty-one etchings." → "some forty
  painted self-portraits and thirty-one etched", or "around eighty self-portraits across all media".

- **Jan van Eyck** — "active from the 1420s **in Bruges** as court painter and confidant to Philip the
  Good". He took employment in The Hague c. 1422 under John of Bavaria, was appointed to Philip the
  Good after 1425 and worked in Lille, "before moving to Bruges in 1429" (Wikipedia, *Jan van Eyck*).
  → "active from the 1420s and settled in Bruges from 1429".

- **Paul Cézanne** — "after early struggles in Paris **alongside Pissarro**". The Cézanne–Pissarro
  working partnership was at Pontoise and Auvers-sur-Oise in the early 1870s, not in Paris. Same
  sentence-run: "**Long ridiculed at the Salon**" — Cézanne was almost invariably *rejected* by the
  Salon (a single acceptance, 1882), so he was rarely in it to be ridiculed. → "after early years in
  Paris, then working beside Pissarro at Pontoise"; "Long rejected by the Salon".

## Questionable / needs a second look

- **Leonardo da Vinci** — `| Salvator Mundi | c. 1500 |` — listed without qualification. Attribution is
  live-disputed ("attributed in whole or part to ... Leonardo", Wikipedia), and the conventional date
  range is c. 1499–1510. Given the card's own "He finished very few paintings", a "(attributed)" tag
  would be more honest.

- **Hieronymus Bosch** — `| The Seven Deadly Sins and the Four Last Things | c. 1500 |` — the Bosch
  Research and Conservation Project called it a follower's work in 2015; the Prado disputes that. Not
  wrong, but it is the second contested row on a card that boasts about a small accepted corpus.

- **Hieronymus Bosch** — `| The Last Judgement | c. 1482–1516 |` — a 34-year "range" is really "we don't
  know"; Wikipedia gives c. 1482 / "created after 1482". Tighten or write "after 1482".

- **Francisco Goya** — `| The Colossus | c. 1808–1812 |` — the Prado reattributed it in 2008 to Goya's
  assistant Asensio Juliá; Wikipedia now says "traditionally attributed to Francisco de Goya". Worth
  an "(attributed)".

- **Titian** — `| Pastoral Concert | c. 1509 |` — fine as given (Wikipedia attributes it to Titian), but
  it is the Louvre *Concert Champêtre*, long given to Giorgione and still argued over. Flagging only
  so a reviewer knows it is a judgement call, not an oversight.

- **Titian** — `| Venus of Urbino | 1534 |` — defensible (Wikipedia: "begun anywhere from 1532 or 1534,
  and was perhaps completed in 1534"), but the Uffizi and most literature give 1538, the sale date.
  A bare "1534" reads more settled than the scholarship is.

- **Titian** — "active in Venice from about 1508 until he **died of plague**". Titian died on 27 August
  1576 during a plague epidemic; the cause is usually given as fever/old age rather than plague itself.
  → "until his death during the plague of 1576".

- **Raphael** — `| The Fire in the Borgo | 1514 |` — Wikipedia's infobox reads "Giulio Romano, following
  original design by Raphael", year 1514–1517. Design is Raphael's, execution largely workshop, and the
  date is a range. → "1514–1517".

- **Peter Paul Rubens** — `| The Three Graces | 1635 |` — the Prado picture is conventionally 1630–1635.
  A single year is more precise than the source supports.

- **Artemisia Gentileschi** — `| Corisca and the Satyr | c. 1630–1635 |` — Wikipedia gives c. 1635–37.
  Minor, but off at both ends.

- **Claude Monet** — `primary`: "Water Lilies, Haystacks, **Women in the Garden**". The first two are
  right; *Women in the Garden* is a genuine Monet but is not among his three best-known works and does
  not belong in a three-slot shortlist. *Impression, Sunrise* — which the card's own biography singles
  out as the painting that named the movement — is the obvious replacement.

- **Katsushika Hokusai** — `primary` third slot is "Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji", a print series, not
  a single work, and its most famous sheet (*The Great Wave*) already occupies slot one. Same pattern
  on the Hiroshige card, where two of three primaries are series.

- **Category slip across several cards** — the column header is "Painting", but rows include drawings
  (Leonardo, *Vitruvian Man*), sculpture (Degas, *The Little Fourteen-Year-Old Dancer*), print series
  (Goya, *The Disasters of War*; *The Sleep of Reason Produces Monsters*) and whole woodblock series
  (Hokusai, Hiroshige). Individually harmless; collectively it makes the column header inaccurate.
  → Rename the column "Work", or footnote the non-paintings.

- **Eugène Delacroix** — "his **colour theory** directly inspired the Impressionists, Van Gogh and
  Cézanne". Delacroix had no formal colour theory — he had practice, and a journal. "Directly" also
  overstates a line of influence that mostly runs through Signac's later reading of him. → "his handling
  of colour inspired ...".

- **Claude Monet** — "**the** founder and most consistent practitioner of Impressionism". Definite article
  on "founder" writes Renoir, Pissarro, Sisley and Bazille out of the story; "most consistent
  practitioner" is an unfalsifiable superlative. → "a founder of Impressionism and the painter who
  stayed closest to its programme".

## Prose issues

- **Michelangelo** — "yet his frescoes for the Sistine Chapel, the ceiling of 1508–1512 and the later
  Last Judgment, redefined what painting could attempt". The appositive is fenced with commas that also
  read as list separators, so the sentence collapses on first pass. → em-dashes, or recast.

- **Michelangelo** — "His muscular, sculptural figures and the quality contemporaries called *terribilità*
  dominated European art for centuries". A *quality* cannot dominate European art; the compound subject
  strains. Also "for centuries" is doing a lot of unsupported work.

- **Raphael** — "the **third giant** of the High Renaissance alongside Leonardo and Michelangelo". Reads as
  a league table and, faintly, as a demotion. → "the third of the great High Renaissance masters".

- **Raphael** — "He died at thirty-seven, and his balanced style became the model for academic painting
  into the nineteenth century." Two unrelated facts welded with "and".

- **Titian** — "qualities that shaped Rubens, Velázquez and Rembrandt and **made oil paint itself
  expressive**". The closing clause is a flourish that does not survive scrutiny — oil paint had been
  expressive for a century by 1550. Also two "and"s in one clause.

- **Titian / Caravaggio** — near-identical stock list in consecutive-ish cards: Titian "shaped Rubens,
  Velázquez and Rembrandt"; Caravaggio "shaping Rubens, Velázquez, Rembrandt and Georges de La Tour".
  Vary one of them.

- **Jan van Eyck** — "made him **the most influential painter** in fifteenth-century northern Europe".
  Unhedged superlative in the same sentence as "effectively launched the Northern Renaissance", so the
  card makes two very large claims back to back. Keep one.

- **Recurring across five cards** — the neglect-then-rediscovery closer is the card set's tic:
  Bosch "Long regarded as an eccentric curiosity"; Vermeer "largely forgotten until the critic
  Théophile Thoré-Bürger revived his reputation"; El Greco "Dismissed for centuries as bizarre, he was
  **rediscovered around 1900**"; Friedrich "forgotten by his death, he was **rediscovered around 1900**";
  Artemisia "**Long overlooked**, she is now recognised as one of the finest painters of her generation".
  The El Greco and Friedrich pair share the identical phrase. Rewrite at least those two.

- **Artemisia Gentileschi** — "she is now recognised as one of the finest painters of her generation" is
  the hollow-praise register: it asserts standing without saying anything. The preceding sentence about
  Judith already earns the point.

- **Peter Paul Rubens** — "he remained **influential from Watteau and Delacroix to Renoir**". As written,
  the influence has a date range rather than an object. → "he remained influential on painters from
  Watteau and Delacroix to Renoir".

- **Jacques-Louis David** — "supplied the moral vocabulary of the Revolution, **in which he served as a
  deputy** and voted for the king's execution". One serves as a deputy in an assembly, not in a
  revolution; the relative pronoun lands on the wrong noun. → "of the Revolution; he sat as a deputy in
  the Convention and voted for the king's execution".

- **Caspar David Friedrich** — "placing solitary **Rückenfiguren seen from behind**" is a tautology
  (a *Rückenfigur* is by definition seen from behind). If the gloss is wanted, punctuate it as one:
  "Rückenfiguren — figures seen from behind". Same sentence, "the defining **image-maker** of German
  Romanticism" is an odd register slip in an otherwise formal paragraph.

- **Utagawa Hiroshige** — "**Celebrated for** atmospheric weather, snow, rain and mist, and for bold
  cropping, **his prints** were copied in oil by Van Gogh". Dangling modifier: the participle wants
  Hiroshige, but the sentence hands it his prints. → "Celebrated for ... , he had his prints copied in
  oil by Van Gogh", or "His prints, celebrated for ... , were copied ...".

- **Édouard Manet** — "**Though he refused to exhibit with the Impressionists**, he was **their
  acknowledged leader** and friend". The concessive does not rescue the contradiction, and
  "leader and friend" yokes two unlike nouns. → "he never exhibited with the Impressionists, but they
  took him as their example".

- **Pierre-Auguste Renoir** — "**Crippled** by rheumatoid arthritis, he painted almost to the day he
  died." Dated register for an encyclopedia opening. → "Severely disabled by rheumatoid arthritis".

- **Paul Cézanne** — "he was hailed by Matisse and Picasso as the father of modern art **and the direct
  ancestor of Cubism**". The second half is a modern art-historical verdict, not something Matisse and
  Picasso said; the sentence attributes it to them.

- **Leonardo da Vinci** — "Active from about 1472 until **his death in France**, ... working in Florence,
  Milan, Rome **and Amboise**." The France fact is stated twice in one sentence.

- **British/US spelling (low priority)** — the set is British throughout (colour, draughtsman, idealisation,
  moralising, recognised), which is consistent. The one inconsistency is *Judgment* vs *Judgement*:
  Michelangelo's card uses "The Last Judgment" (×3), while van Eyck and Bosch use "Last Judgement" and
  Rubens has "The Judgement of Paris". Each matches a defensible title convention, so this is a house-style
  call rather than a mistake.
